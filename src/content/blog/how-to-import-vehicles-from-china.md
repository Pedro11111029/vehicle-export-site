Importing vehicles from China is a repeatable process, but the details differ from importing consumer goods. Vehicles are high-value, regulated, and usually require both a shipping method and a compliance file. This guide walks through the sequence we use with first-time importers, from the first quotation to the vehicle clearing customs at your port.

## 1. Define the specification before you ask for a price

A quotation is only meaningful once these are fixed:

- **Destination country and port** — freight, duties and homologation all depend on it.
- **Steering side** — left-hand drive (LHD) or right-hand drive (RHD).
- **Powertrain** — petrol, diesel, hybrid or battery-electric.
- **Emission standard** — Euro III through Euro VI, or the local equivalent.
- **Volume** — a single sample unit, a container load, or a rolling programme.

Sellers who quote before knowing these will either over-price to protect themselves or under-price and adjust later. Expect a serious supplier to ask all five questions before sending a price. You can see the configurations we supply in our [product categories](/products/).

## 2. Understand what the price does and does not include

Export prices are usually quoted on one of three terms:

| Term | Supplier pays | Buyer pays |
| --- | --- | --- |
| FOB (Free On Board) | Vehicle to the named Chinese port, export clearance | Ocean freight, insurance, import clearance |
| CIF (Cost, Insurance, Freight) | The above plus freight and marine insurance to your port | Import duties, taxes, local delivery |
| DDP (Delivered Duty Paid) | Everything to your address | Nothing beyond the agreed price |

Most first-time buyers are quoted FOB without realising it, then are surprised by the freight invoice. Ask explicitly which term the price uses, and request a CIF number as well so you can compare landed cost.

> Rule of thumb: for a 20-foot container to West Africa or South America, ocean freight can equal 10–25% of the vehicle value. Always compare landed cost, not FOB.

## 3. Verify the supplier, not just the product

Before transferring a deposit:

1. Ask for the business licence and confirm the registered company name matches the bank account name.
2. Prefer payment to the **company account**, never to a personal account.
3. Request a video call walking through the yard or factory.
4. Ask for two or three references in your region, and actually call them.
5. For larger orders, arrange a third-party inspection (SGS, BV or Intertek) before shipment.

A supplier who resists any of these is a risk signal, regardless of how good the price looks.

## 4. Documents you should receive

A complete export file typically contains:

- **Commercial invoice** and **packing list**
- **Bill of Lading (B/L)** — original or telex release, depending on the agreement
- **Certificate of Origin** (usually Form E or a general CO)
- **Vehicle documents** — chassis number, engine number, manufacture date
- **Export licence** for vehicles where required
- **UN38.3 test summary and MSDS** for battery-electric vehicles
- **Insurance certificate** if shipping on CIF terms

Check that the VIN, model and quantity on the invoice match the B/L and the physical units. Mismatches at this stage are much cheaper to fix than at the destination port.

## 5. Shipping: container or RoRo?

Two methods dominate:

- **Container (20' or 40' HQ)** — vehicles are driven or forklifted inside and secured. A 40' HQ fits two to four cars depending on size. Good for mixed loads, high-value units and destinations without RoRo service.
- **RoRo (Roll-on/Roll-off)** — vehicles are driven onto a dedicated vessel. Usually cheaper per unit for standard cars and trucks, but you need a port with RoRo service and the schedule is less flexible.

Electric vehicles are a special case: carriers treat them as Class 9 dangerous goods, so some RoRo operators refuse them or charge a surcharge. Container shipping with the battery disconnected and documentation prepared is often the more reliable route — see our [electric trucks](/products/electric-trucks/) for the models and battery options we export.

## 6. Budget for the costs outside the invoice

First-time importers commonly forget:

- **Import duty and VAT** at the destination (varies from 0% to over 50%)
- **Homologation or type approval** costs if you plan to register the vehicles
- **Port charges, demurrage and storage** if clearance is delayed
- **Inland transport** from port to your yard
- **Pre-delivery inspection (PDI)** and any reconditioning
- **Currency movement** between quotation and payment

A realistic landed-cost model adds 10–35% to the FOB price depending on the market. Build that into your retail or resale calculation before you commit.

## 7. Common mistakes to avoid

1. **Paying a 100% deposit upfront.** Standard terms are 30% deposit, 70% before shipment — or a letter of credit for large orders.
2. **Skipping the pre-shipment inspection** to save a few hundred dollars.
3. **Accepting a B/L with the wrong consignee name.** Corrections after departure are slow and expensive.
4. **Ordering a specification that cannot be registered** in the destination market.
5. **Ignoring the spare parts plan.** A fleet without a parts supply agreement loses money every time a vehicle is off the road.
